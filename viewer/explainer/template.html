How the pen works

You hold a soft sleeve that stays still. Inside it, the front of the pen can tilt, so the ink tip moves by itself, up to 3 mm. Proposed design It moves against your shake, or toward the letter you mean. A gentle buzz gives cues, and the phone app helps with spelling.

Steadier ink

Sensors feel the shake. The nose tilts the other way, so the ink stays near your letters.

Bigger writing

When your letters start to shrink, the pen buzzes: a reminder to write bigger.

Practice letters

The tip nudges toward the letter shape. A desk board can pull the pen along whole letters.

Spelling help

The app reads your writing, flags a misspelt word with a buzz, and shows and reads the right spelling.

      How you hold a pen, and where the shake comes from

      The pen has to work with the way people already write. That is why the fingers hold a part that stays still.

      Most people hold a pen with three fingers: the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger. This is the “tripod” grip. The pen rests in the web between the thumb and the index finger.

      The fingers make the small up-down strokes and loops. The wrist makes strokes and the slant. The arm moves the hand along the line.

      A shake from the wrist and forearm moves the hand, the fingers and the whole pen together. So the pen cannot rely on your fingers staying still. It has to move its own tip.

      What a pen can and cannot do

      A pen cannot push your hand: there is nothing for it to push against. But it can move its own tip a few millimetres against the hand, and it can give you cues.

      The guidance board sits on the desk, so it can push.

      Handwriting movements are mostly slower than 5 per second. Literature · CON-24
      Essential tremor: 4 to 12 per second. Literature · PDT-07, PDT-31
      Parkinson's rest tremor: 4 to 6 per second. It usually fades while writing. Literature · PDT-07, PDT-09

      The average essential tremor is about 6 per second, and it gets slower with age. Literature · PDT-31 That puts most tremor right in the overlap, which is why telling shake from writing is the hardest part and still has to be tested on real writing.

      Why the fingers hold a sleeve that stays still

      If the fingers held the moving part, they would feel every correction and fight it. With a fixed sleeve, the moving nose is inside, out of the way.

      A ring at the front of the sleeve rests on the paper and takes your writing pressure. So the moving tip only has to steer, not push.

      The nose tilts on a pivot 45 mm from the tip. The magnets sit behind the pivot, so a small push at the back swings the tip the other way. Proposed design

      The ball tip can move up to 3 mm from its centre. Proposed design That is about the size of a moderate tremor: tablet studies of essential tremor found a typical shake of about 2 mm from side to side in spiral drawing. Literature · PDT-12 Larger shakes are only partly corrected.

      Limits, and what we still need to test

      What it cannot do

      • It cannot write for you or fix spelling on the paper. The app can flag a word and show the right spelling.
      • It cannot push your hand. Only the desk board can pull the pen along a letter, and it cannot overpower you: it pulls at most 0.4 N and gives way if you push against it. Assumption
      • It cannot move the tip more than about 3 mm. Proposed design A bigger shake is only partly corrected.
      • It cannot perfectly tell shake from writing when both have the same rhythm, about 4–6 per second. Literature · CON-24, PDT-31
      • It does not cure tremor or small writing. It helps while you write, and it helps you practise.
      • Extra weight alone is not expected to help Parkinson's tremor. Literature · ACT-32

      What we still need to test

      • Build the moving nose and measure how far and how fast it moves while writing on real paper.
      • Record real handwriting from people with essential tremor and Parkinson's (after ethics approval), and check that the tracker can tell shake from writing.
      • Find out how the pen feels: weight, balance, the ring on the paper, noise, warmth of the coils, battery life.
      • Test the “write bigger” buzz with people with Parkinson's: do letters stay bigger, and does writing stay fluent? Literature · PDT-17
      • Test guided practice without the pen afterwards: does writing stay better once the help is removed? Literature · HAP-12
      • Test the board first with a mechanical “hand” and then with writers: does a gentle pull lead a relaxed hand along the letter, and does it give way when pushed?
      • Measure how people hold and turn the pen while writing. This decides whether the product keeps the rear inertial module that is fitted in the first prototype.
      • Check magnet safety for people with implants (pacemakers, deep-brain stimulators) before any board or pen trial.
      • Test the spelling help with dyslexic writers: the cues must help, not distract.
